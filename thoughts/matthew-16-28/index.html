“Some standing here”: what did Jesus mean in Matthew 16:28?

Everyone who heard that sentence is dead. Jesus has not returned. So was he wrong? There are four main answers, and each one strains somewhere.

Study · October 2026

“Truly, I say to you, there are some standing here who will not taste death until they see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom.” — Matthew 16:28, ESV

Bertrand Russell thought Jesus was wrong. In Why I Am Not a Christian he pointed to this verse as one of several places where Jesus seems to expect his return within his hearers’ lifetimes. Albert Schweitzer argued something similar in 1906, and Bart Ehrman does today.

A popular reply online says the answer is in the next sentence: the Transfiguration. It’s a real reading, and not a silly one. It’s also not the whole story.

Reading 1: The Transfiguration

The case is simple.

  • Matthew, Mark, and Luke all place the saying right before the Transfiguration (Matthew 17:1–2; Mark 9:1–2; Luke 9:27–28).
  • Matthew and Mark say “after six days.” Luke says “about eight days.”
  • Peter, James, and John go up the mountain and see Jesus in glory.
  • Decades later, 2 Peter 1:16–18 recalls being “eyewitnesses of his majesty” on “the holy mountain,” and calls it the “power and coming” of Jesus.

A side note. You’ll sometimes hear that the chapter break is the problem: a medieval editor split the text, and skeptics stopped reading. The chapter divisions are medieval. They trace mostly to Stephen Langton in the early 1200s, and verse numbers came in the 1500s. But the argument doesn’t need that point. The Gospel writers themselves put the two scenes together, and that is the strongest thing this reading has.

Where it strains:

  • “Will not taste death until.” That wording suggests some would die before it happened. A week is a strange window for that. The ESV Reformation Study Bible makes the same point.
  • “Coming in his kingdom.” That’s big language for a private vision shared by three men. And the verse just before it (16:27) describes the Son of Man coming with his angels in his Father’s glory to repay each person. That sounds like judgment.
  • Placement isn’t proof. The writers put the scenes side by side. That shows they connected them. It doesn’t show that one is meant to fulfill the other.

The Transfiguration may well be a preview of the glory. It’s a stretch to say it settles what Jesus meant.

Reading 2: The kingdom arrives in stages

Some commentators read “coming in his kingdom” as the whole sequence: resurrection, ascension, the sending of the Spirit. All of it happened within the disciples’ lifetimes. The ESV Reformation Study Bible leans this way and treats the Transfiguration as possibly the first event in that sequence.

This is not a fringe puzzle. Davies and Allison, in their Matthew commentary, survey about eight proposals for this one saying.

Reading 3: Vindication

This is the reading of N. T. Wright and R. T. France, and it turns on what “coming” means.

In Daniel 7:13, “one like a son of man” comes with the clouds to the Ancient of Days. He moves toward God to receive a kingdom, not down to earth. Wright and France argue that when Jesus uses that language, he means vindication and enthronement.

Mark 14:62 fits the idea. Jesus tells the high priest, “you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven.” He says “you will see” to a man who was alive at the time.

Wright reads Mark 13 as being about the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70 and uses it to interpret the other “coming” sayings. He is firm that this is not the Second Coming. In Surprised by Hope he says the Second Coming has not yet happened.

Jeannine Brown argues something similar for Matthew. She notes that the Greek translation of Daniel 7 uses the same verb Matthew uses here (erchomai), and that Matthew has a different word, parousia, for a physical arrival.

The objections are serious.

  • Verse 27. The verse before sounds like final judgment. A Ligonier article that defends the Daniel 7 reading takes this on directly: it reads verse 27 as part of the same Daniel 7 judgment scene, and a footnote allows a secondary reference to the final judgment. Kenneth Gentry splits it differently: verse 27 is the final judgment, and verse 28 is a nearer coming at AD 70.
  • Other passages. Matthew 24–25, Acts 1:11, and 1 Thessalonians 4:16 describe a return from heaven. Edward Adams argued in the Tyndale Bulletin (2005) that Mark’s coming sayings do refer to Jesus’ return. William Lane Craig argues the coming is a visible arrival on earth.

Reading 4: Jesus was wrong

Schweitzer thought Jesus expected the kingdom to arrive almost at once, before the disciples even returned from the mission in Matthew 10 (see verse 23). When it didn’t, Jesus concluded his own death would bring it. On Schweitzer’s view, Jesus was mistaken.

Ehrman describes Jesus as an apocalyptic prophet who expected judgment and the kingdom within his own generation. He lands in the same place.

This reading has an honest advantage: it takes “some standing here” at face value. Its weakness, as Michael Bird points out, is a question: if most of the disciples had already died when Mark and Matthew wrote, why would they keep a saying that supposedly proved Jesus wrong?

Does the word “coming” settle it?

No. Erchomai is an ordinary verb of motion: come, go. Daniel 7 gives it one direction, toward God. Other New Testament texts give it another, toward earth. Context decides, and here the context is mixed.

Where that leaves me

I find the Daniel 7 reading strongest on the word “coming,” and verse 27 still bothers me. The Transfiguration reading is legitimate, but it asks “taste death” to mean less than it says. The “Jesus was wrong” reading is the most literal, and it deserves a real answer, not a dismissal.

One verse can’t carry this alone. The bigger question, whether Jesus expected an imminent end, runs through Mark 13, Matthew 24, and Matthew 10:23 too.

If an answer to this verse sounds easy and complete, be suspicious of it.
