Bible & early Christianity

How is it we don’t know what happened to Paul?

Acts spends chapters getting Paul to Rome. He finally arrives. He is still alive. And Luke basically says, “Anyway, that’s enough about that.”

Study · September 2026

This is one of those things that becomes stranger the longer you think about it. Paul is arguably the best documented individual in the earliest Christian movement after Jesus, and Acts simply stops while he is still alive.

Acts 28 ends with Paul in Rome under a form of house arrest. He has been there for two years. He is receiving visitors and preaching openly. There is no verdict, no execution, no death scene, and no neat apostolic curtain call.

“He lived there two whole years at his own expense, and welcomed all who came to him … with all openness, unhindered.” — Acts 28:30–31

Then: end of Acts.

Which is conspicuous. Acts is not shy about death. Stephen’s killing is narrated in detail. James the son of Zebedee is executed by Herod. Luke knows how to tell us when somebody dies.

So what do we actually know?

1. Acts does not tell us.

The New Testament narrative gets Paul to Rome and leaves him there. That is the last scene. Whatever happened afterward has to be reconstructed from later evidence.

2. The earliest post-New-Testament witness points to martyrdom.

First Clement, usually dated near the end of the first century, speaks of Paul as someone who endured repeated suffering, bore witness before rulers, and then “departed from the world.” The language is brief and not a newspaper account, but it is early—within living memory of Paul’s generation.

That makes Paul’s martyrdom substantially more than a late medieval legend. What it does not give us is a detailed eyewitness account.

3. The familiar story is that Paul was beheaded in Rome under Nero.

By the early fourth century, Eusebius records an established Christian tradition that Paul was beheaded in Rome and Peter was crucified there under Nero. Eusebius also points to older Roman memorial traditions associated with both apostles.

That is good evidence for a strong and early tradition. It is not the same thing as having a surviving trial transcript, execution record, or account by someone who stood there and watched it happen.

The useful distinction:

We have good early evidence that Paul died as a martyr. We have much less certainty about the exact sequence of events, date, location, and manner of death.

And then there is the second-imprisonment theory.

Eusebius also preserves a tradition that Paul was released after the imprisonment at the end of Acts, resumed his ministry, later returned to Rome, and was imprisoned again before his death.

That would fit naturally with the tone of 2 Timothy, where Paul sounds like a man who believes the end is close: “the time of my departure has come.”

But this reconstruction is more contested than it sometimes sounds. It depends partly on later tradition and partly on how one dates and understands the Pastoral Epistles. It is plausible. It is not established.

So the common reconstruction looks something like this:

Rome → first imprisonment → possible release → more ministry → second arrest → martyrdom under Nero.

The problem is that the arrows become more speculative as you move to the right.

Why would Luke stop there?

One possibility is simply that Acts was written before Paul died. That gives you a very tidy explanation: Luke stopped because history had not happened yet.

The difficulty is that many scholars date Acts later than Paul’s death, often around the last decades of the first century. If that dating is right, Luke probably knew at least that Paul was dead.

The stronger literary explanation is that Luke ended Acts where his story had reached its destination.

Acts begins with a program: the message will move from Jerusalem outward, eventually to “the ends of the earth.” In Luke’s world, Rome is the symbolic center of the nations. By the last sentence, Paul is preaching there openly and “unhindered.”

In other words, the ending may be less:

“What happened to Paul?”

and more:

“Look where the gospel got.”

If that is Luke’s purpose, Paul’s biography has served it. His personal ending is no longer the ending of the book.

But why didn’t somebody else write it down?

Because first-century Christianity was not yet an institution with a historical archive department and an assistant vice president of apostolic records retention.

Paul was important to Christians. To the Roman state, he was one prisoner among an enormous population. If there were official records of a hearing or execution, there is no reason to expect them to have been copied and preserved for two thousand years.

The Christian communities that cared about him were comparatively small and vulnerable. The period in which the details were easiest to recover was precisely the period in which Christians had the least institutional capacity to preserve them.

By the time the church was producing fuller histories, some facts remained and some specifics had already become tradition.

Were the other apostles better documented?

Mostly, no.

That is the part that corrects the original question. We often feel as though we know what happened to the Twelve because later Christian tradition gives nearly every apostle a memorable ending.

Historically, the New Testament records very few of those deaths. James the son of Zebedee is executed in Acts. Judas dies before that story really gets moving. Beyond them, the familiar deaths of Andrew, Thomas, Bartholomew, and others come mostly from later traditions of very uneven historical value.

Peter and Paul stand out because Rome remembered them. James the brother of Jesus stands out because Josephus mentions his death outside Christian literature. In general, an apostle’s death is more likely to be preserved when a literate community had a reason, an opportunity, and an institution capable of remembering it.

So how is it we don’t know what happened to Paul?

Because nobody who saw his final days left us a surviving account.

What remains is a strong early tradition that he died as a martyr, almost certainly connected with Rome, and a later tradition that he was beheaded under Nero. The exact route between Acts 28 and that ending is mostly dark.

Which leaves one of the great narrative irritations in the New Testament:

Paul spends a large part of Acts insisting, “I appeal to Caesar.”

Acts spends chapters getting him to Caesar.

He finally reaches Rome.

And Luke:

Anyway, that’s enough about that.
